\section{Introduction}
%% ======================================================================

\IEEEPARstart{A}{n} agent that runs for weeks
\cite{yao2023react,wang2024agentsurvey} accumulates a history far larger than
any context window \cite{brown2020gpt3}, so something must decide what enters the prompt each turn. A longer
window does not remove the problem: accuracy falls 30--50\% well before the
documented limit across frontier models \cite{chroma2025contextrot}, and
position inside a long context changes how much the model uses
\cite{liu2024lostmiddle}. These are properties of attention over long inputs,
not gaps in training, so a larger window moves the difficulty rather than
solving it. The limiting factor is curation, not capacity.

The cost of poor curation can be measured. On LongMemEval, oracle retrieval
scores about 92\% where the same model retrieving for itself scores about 58\%
\cite{wu2024longmemeval}. The model answers correctly once the right evidence
is placed in front of it, so this 34-point difference is a selection failure
rather than a reasoning failure.

Systems that address selection already exist, and agent architectures now
treat memory as a first-class module \cite{sumers2024coala}. Tiered memory with summarisation
and forgetting is used in MemGPT \cite{packer2023memgpt}, MemoryBank
\cite{zhong2024memorybank} and the architectures reviewed in
\cite{zhang2024memorysurvey}. What has not been established is whether the
individual mechanisms inside those systems earn their cost, since they are
evaluated as complete systems rather than as separate decisions. A recent
survey states the gap directly: current systems ``do not solve the fundamental
challenge: deciding what to remember and what to forget''
\cite{zhang2024memorysurvey}. A rate--distortion framing \cite{shannon1959rd,cover2006elements} has been
proposed for this family of decisions \cite{colaco2026rd}, but it provides an analytical
framework rather than a measured system.

This paper measures the decision. Our research question is: how much of the
memory-selection gap can a better decision policy recover, and under what
budget does compression become worthwhile?

Our contributions are as follows. (1)~A storage-budgeted protocol correcting
an accounting error common in this area: bounding only the assembled context
charges a store-everything baseline nothing for retention, settling the
comparison before it is run (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). (2)~Evidence that
the gain is selection, not compression --- at identical storage, fidelity and
retrieval, scored forgetting is worth $+9.37$ answer-recall points over FIFO
while compressing an evicted turn loses $7.13$ (Sections~\ref{sec:selection}
and~\ref{sec:significance}). (3)~A test of the rate--distortion crossover, to
$182\times$ compression (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}). (4)~Byte-denominated
accounting: the embedding index outweighs the text it indexes by about
$12\times$ and reverses the policy ranking below roughly 40\,KiB
(Section~\ref{sec:bytes}). (5)~A negative result for LLM-judged salience --- as
a single-component swap, a 0.5B judge loses to a hand-written heuristic by
$5.87$ points (Section~\ref{sec:scorer}). (6)~A learned scorer trained on two
corpora, one LLM-generated and one written by people, with the direction in
which it transfers and the direction in which it does not
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). (7)~An end-task evaluation through a
fixed reader, measuring how much of the recall advantage becomes correct
answers (Section~\ref{sec:endtask}). (8)~Comparisons with MemGPT-style and
Mem0-style memory rebuilt in the same harness (Section~\ref{sec:external}).
(9)~A scale test on chats of up to ten million tokens, and a scorer retrained
on 14.6 times the data, including the finding that more data alone did not
help (Sections~\ref{sec:scale} and~\ref{sec:retrain}). (10)~A working
application built on the same code paths as the experiments, with the scoring
rules and equations it runs (Sections~\ref{sec:equations}
and~\ref{sec:system}). (11)~Three measurement errors in our own results, with
the checks that caught them (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

Five of these eleven results are negative or corrective. We consider this a useful
outcome. Described only as a three-tier memory layer, the system would
duplicate work that has already been done; measured against controls, it
indicates which parts of that design actually pay for themselves.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ======================================================================

MemGPT \cite{packer2023memgpt} pages a virtual context against external storage
under the model's own control; MemoryBank \cite{zhong2024memorybank} adds an
Ebbinghaus forgetting curve; Generative Agents \cite{park2023generative} score
memories by recency, importance and relevance; A-MEM \cite{xu2025amem}
organises them as interlinked notes, and Mem0 \cite{chhikara2025mem0}
extracts and updates facts with an LLM. The design echoes the multi-store
model of human memory \cite{atkinson1968memory}. This work shows tiering and forgetting are
practical, but because these systems are evaluated end to end, the contribution
of any single decision inside them cannot be separated from the pipeline. Our
harness is built to make that separation possible.

A second line compresses the prompt or the attention cache rather than choosing
between stored items: LLMLingua \cite{jiang2023llmlingua} prunes tokens by
perplexity, Gist tokens \cite{mu2023gist} and AutoCompressors
\cite{chevalier2023autocompressor} learn dense summaries, the in-context autoencoder \cite{ge2024icae} compresses a context into memory
slots, and StreamingLLM
\cite{xiao2024streaming} and H\textsubscript{2}O \cite{zhang2023h2o} evict from
the KV cache by attention-derived scores. Architectural memories --- Transformer
memories that compress \cite{rae2020compressive} or index
\cite{wu2022memorizing,wang2023longmem} past activations --- change the model
itself. All operate within a single forward
pass over a contiguous context, or require training the model. The decision studied here is upstream and
persistent: it fixes which items survive across sessions, where an evicted item
cannot be recovered from the cache at all.

Retrieval augmentation \cite{lewis2020rag,karpukhin2020dpr,borgeaud2022retro}
is the store-everything baseline these systems are measured against, and the
RAG arm of our harness. Choosing what to evict from a bounded store is also
the classical page-replacement problem
\cite{belady1966replacement,denning1968workingset}, with the difference that
the future accesses here are questions no policy can see.

LoCoMo \cite{maharana2024locomo} supplies multi-session dialogues annotated
with gold evidence turn identifiers, which is what lets a retrieval-level
metric be computed without a generative model; LongMemEval
\cite{wu2024longmemeval} isolates several memory abilities and is the source of
the gap quoted above, and LongBench \cite{bai2024longbench} measures
long-context understanding without a memory layer. REALTALK \cite{lee2025realtalk} applies LoCoMo's
annotation schema to 21 days of messaging between real people, which makes it
the natural test of whether a scorer fitted on LoCoMo learned what matters or
only what LLM-generated dialogue looks like. We propose neither a new architecture nor a new
benchmark: the contribution is a measurement protocol and its results,
including a case where correcting the protocol reverses the conclusion.

\section{The MemGate Harness}
\label{sec:harness}
%% ======================================================================

MemGate is placed between an agent and a frozen LLM, and controls only what
enters the prompt (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The base model is never trained or
fine-tuned and is treated as a black box, as in retrieval augmentation
\cite{lewis2020rag}, so any difference we report can be
attributed to the memory policy.

\subsection{Tiers as a lifecycle}
The store has three tiers, but they are organised as a lifecycle rather than as
a partition. Every turn enters Tier~1 verbatim, and the routing decision is
taken once, at eviction, when the budget binds. Writing $u$ for the utility
assigned to an item by the scorer:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:route}
\begin{array}{ll}
u \geq \tau_{\text{fact}} & \rightarrow\ \text{Tier 3: atomic fact}+\text{vector}\\
u \geq \tau_{\text{low}}  & \rightarrow\ \text{Tier 2: compressed gist}\\
\text{otherwise}          & \rightarrow\ \text{dropped}
\end{array}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_{\text{fact}}=0.45$ and $\tau_{\text{low}}=0.15$; an item with
$u\ge\tau_{\text{fact}}+0.2$ also leaves a gist in Tier~2.
The tiers follow the working-set \cite{denning1968workingset} and multi-store
\cite{atkinson1968memory} views of memory.
Tier~1 holds recent verbatim turns and answers the question \emph{when};
Tier~2 holds compressed gists and answers \emph{gist}; Tier~3 holds durable
facts with embeddings, answers \emph{what}, and enters the context only when
retrieval matches. Scoring each item once, at eviction, is what makes the
decision point separable, since it gives a single site in the pipeline that a
controlled swap can target.

\subsection{Policies}
The arms are P0 (sliding window, recency, verbatim), RAG (store-all, FIFO, verbatim), P1 (threshold, scored at ingest), P1-A (adaptive: score orders eviction, compress), P1-S (select: score orders eviction, drop), P2 (0.5B judge), P3 (learned, never fitted on the conversation it scores) and an oracle. Two require comment. RAG is the
no-policy control: it stores everything, retrieves the top $k$ items, and
evicts FIFO when the store is full. It is not a weak baseline but the arm that
a decision policy has to beat in order to have contributed anything. P0 is
given the same token budget as MemGate, so the only difference between them is
which turns are selected, not how many tokens may be spent.

\subsection{Scoring, routing and the storage budget}
\label{sec:equations}

Every equation in this subsection is transcribed from the code that produced
the results: the modules \texttt{scoring}, \texttt{pretrained},
\texttt{ingest}, \texttt{store} and \texttt{policies}, and the two training
scripts.
A message $m$ is always scored with its speaker tag removed, and the scorer
sees its text and speaker only: no question, evidence label or future turn.

\emph{The P1 heuristic.} Let $W(m)$ be the words of $m$, $D$ a list of 28
durability cues (\emph{deadline, budget, prefer, born, lives, works},
\ldots), and $h(m)=|W(m)\cap D|$. With indicators $\delta_{\mathrm{num}}$ (a
digit), $\delta_{\mathrm{tmp}}$ (an amount of money, or a month prefix),
$\delta_{\mathrm{prop}}$ (a capitalised word not opening a sentence),
$\delta_{?}$ (ends with a question mark), $\delta_{\mathrm{asst}}$ (written
by the assistant) and $\delta_{\mathrm{upd}}$ (a correction cue such as
\emph{actually} or \emph{no longer} together with a digit or month),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:p1}
\begin{gathered}
s(m)=0.15+\min(0.35,\,0.18\,h)+0.20\,\delta_{\mathrm{num}}+0.15\,\delta_{\mathrm{tmp}}\\
\quad{}+0.12\,\delta_{\mathrm{prop}}-0.12\,\delta_{?}-0.10\,\delta_{\mathrm{asst}}+0.10\,\delta_{\mathrm{upd}},\\[2pt]
u_{\mathrm{P1}}(m)=
\begin{cases}
0.02, & m \text{ is filler},\\
\min\big(1,\max(0,\,s(m))\big), & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where a message is filler when it is an
acknowledgement or greeting from a closed list, or has at most two words.
The weights were set by hand and never fitted.

\emph{The learned scorer P3.} Its input concatenates 15 hand features
$\phi(m)$ (word and character counts, digit, month, money, proper noun,
question, final period, $h(m)$, correction cue, filler, assistant speaker,
share of words of at most three letters, capital letters, clock time) with
the 384-dimensional unit-norm MiniLM embedding $e(m)$
\cite{wang2020minilm,reimers2019sbert}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lr}
\begin{gathered}
x(m)=\big[\phi(m);\,e(m)\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{399},\\
z(m)=b+\sum_{j=1}^{399} w_j\,\frac{x_j(m)-\mu_j}{\sigma_j},\quad
u_{\mathrm{P3}}(m)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z(m)}},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $\mu,\sigma$ the training mean and standard deviation of each input. The
label is distant supervision \cite{mintz2009distant}: $y_i=1$ when any question cites turn $i$ as
evidence. With $N$ training turns from corpora $c$ grouped into families
$f\in F$ (LoCoMo, REALTALK, BEAM, MemBench, everyday chat), the fit is
an $\ell_2$-regularised, class-balanced, sample-weighted logistic regression
fitted with scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\begin{gathered}
\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2
 + C\sum_{i}\omega_i\,\kappa_{y_i}\,\ell\big(y_i,\,u_{\mathrm{P3}}(m_i)\big),\\
\omega_i=\frac{N\,\pi_{c(i)}}{|F|\,n_{c(i)}},\qquad
\kappa_y=\frac{N}{2N_y},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the log loss, $C=1$, $n_c$ is the number of turns of corpus
$c$, and $\pi_c$ is the share of its family's weight that corpus $c$ receives:
$n_c/n_f$ (by turns, the recipe of the PR~\#12 model), or $P_c/P_f$ (by
evidence turns $P$, used for the larger model). Every family therefore weighs
the same, whatever its size. Generated small-talk lines enter as negatives
with total weight $0.05N$.

\emph{The shipped ensemble.} The shipped scorer averages the logits of two
such models, $A$ (the PR~\#12 recipe) and $B$ (all 856{,}876 turns). Because
each logit is linear in $x$, the mean is again a single logistic regression,
which is how it is stored (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ens}
\begin{gathered}
\bar z(m)=\tfrac12\big(z_A(m)+z_B(m)\big)=\bar b+\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}x(m),\\
\bar w_j=\tfrac12\sum_{k\in\{A,B\}}\frac{w^{(k)}_j}{\sigma^{(k)}_j},\quad
\bar b=\tfrac12\sum_{k}\Big(b^{(k)}-\sum_j\frac{w^{(k)}_j\mu^{(k)}_j}{\sigma^{(k)}_j}\Big).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
P1-S only orders by $u$, but the thresholds of (\ref{eq:route}) and the
guards below read $u$ as absolute. The logit is therefore mapped by an affine
function that leaves every ranking unchanged, in the manner of Platt scaling
\cite{platt1999probabilistic},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cal}
\begin{gathered}
z'=\alpha\bar z+\beta,\qquad
\beta=\operatorname{logit}\tau_{\text{fact}}-\alpha\,q_{\text{fact}},\\
\alpha=\frac{\operatorname{logit}\tau_{\text{fact}}-\operatorname{logit}\tau_{\text{low}}}{q_{\text{fact}}-q_{\text{low}}},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $q_{\text{fact}}$ and $q_{\text{low}}$ are the quantiles of $\bar z$
over LoCoMo and REALTALK turns that put 36.48\% of them above
$\tau_{\text{fact}}$ and 71.85\% above $\tau_{\text{low}}$, the shares of the
first shipped model.

\emph{The application's guard.} The application wraps its scorer in
GuardedScorer (Fig.~\ref{fig:scoring}). Each guard fires on one kind of input
the scorers were not built for (no words, small talk, code, non-Latin script,
repetition), and ordinary English passes straight through. One further rule
corrects a measured weakness of the learned scorer: trained with BEAM, whose
long answers are full of numbers nobody asks about, it gave a short dated
fact the score of a greeting. For a message of $n(m)\le30$ words that names a
month, a clock time or an amount,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:floor}
u_{\mathrm{G}}(m)=\max\big(u_{\mathrm{P3}}(m),\,u_{\mathrm{P1}}(m)\big),
\end{equation}
and $u_{\mathrm{G}}=u_{\mathrm{P3}}$ otherwise. The experiments never use
the guard.

\emph{The storage budget.} Each stored item $i$ has a cost
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost}
c_i=\begin{cases}
\mathrm{tok}(i) & \text{(tokens)},\\
\mathrm{bytes}(i)+1536\,\mathbb{1}[i \text{ has a vector}] & \text{(bytes)},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
and after every write the store enforces
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budget}
\sum_{i\in T_1\cup T_2\cup T_3} c_i\ \le\ S
\end{equation}
by evicting, while it is violated,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:evict}
v=\arg\min_{i\in T_3}\ (u_i,\,t_i)
\end{equation}
in lexicographic order, $t_i$ being the turn index, so the oldest item breaks
a tie (Fig.~\ref{fig:routing}b). Superseded records are reclaimed before any
eviction. P1 and P1-A demote $v$ to a Tier~2 gist of at most eight words when
that gist costs less than $v$, and drop it otherwise; P1-S always drops it;
when Tier~3 is empty the same rule is applied to Tier~2. Tier~1, the
six-item live buffer, is never evicted. RAG uses $\arg\min_i t_i$, first in
first out, and has no scorer. With equal costs, repeated application of
(\ref{eq:evict}) leaves P1-S holding exactly the highest-utility items seen so
far, and RAG the newest ones, the FIFO rule of page replacement
\cite{belady1966replacement}; this is the only difference between them.

\emph{Context assembly.} For a question $q$ and context budget $B$ (2048
tokens in every experiment), the most recent Tier~1 items are packed into
$\lfloor B/4\rfloor$ tokens. In P1-A and P1-S the remainder is filled
greedily from one pool of Tier~2 and Tier~3 items,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retrieve}
R_k(q)=\operatorname{top}_k\big\{\,i : \cos\!\big(e(q),e(i)\big)>0\,\big\},\qquad k=60,
\end{equation}
ranked by cosine similarity. P1 instead gives Tier~2 a fixed quarter of $B$,
packed by information density, and fills the last half from Tier~3 by
(\ref{eq:retrieve}). When a message asks several questions, each is its own
query and the rankings are interleaved, best of each first.

\emph{Store maintenance.} Three rules keep the store consistent, and none
reads an evaluation label. A write to Tier~3 whose embedding has
$\cos\ge0.92$ with an active fact is folded into that fact instead of being
stored twice. A write the P1 heuristic labels a correction (Section~\ref{sec:equations}:
a correction cue with a digit or month) retires the single most similar active
fact with $\cos\ge0.60$. When Tier~2 outgrows its share, its oldest half is
consolidated: items with $u\ge0.5$ are promoted to Tier~3 verbatim and the
rest are merged into one coarser gist.

\subsection{One variable per comparison}
The experimental design lies in the set of contrasts rather than in the set of
policies. Each contrast holds everything constant except one factor:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Contrast & Held constant & Varies \\
\midrule
P0 vs.\ RAG   & retention, storage & read path \\
RAG vs.\ P1-S & storage, fidelity, retrieval & \textbf{what is forgotten} \\
P1-S vs.\ P1-A& storage, selection & \textbf{fidelity} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The second contrast is the one this paper depends on. RAG and P1-S hold the
same verbatim turns, in the same embedding space, retrieved by the same
procedure, under the same storage cap. The only difference is which turns are
discarded when the cap binds: oldest-first for RAG, lowest-utility-first for
P1-S. Any gap between the two is therefore attributable to the decision policy.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
%% ======================================================================

We report two retrieval-level metrics. Evidence recall, also called strict
recall, is the fraction of questions for which every gold evidence turn reached
the assembled context. Answer recall is the fraction for which the answer text
survived into the context, a string-containment test in the spirit of extractive
QA scoring \cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, computed over the 715 of 1{,}527 questions whose
answer is literally recoverable from the evidence turns.

The distinction is load-bearing. Evidence recall credits an identifier: a
compressed gist retains the identifier of a turn whose text it discarded, and
records a hit with none of the answer behind it. In development we saw evidence
recall rise from 8.1\% to 14.8\% while answer recall stayed at 12.8\% --- the
gain was entirely identifier bookkeeping. Dropping against compressing is
precisely the comparison on which the two diverge, so reporting evidence recall
alone would have produced the opposite conclusion. We treat answer recall as
primary and report evidence recall alongside it to keep the divergence visible.

Both are forms of context recall, an upper bound on end-task accuracy rather
than accuracy itself. Their advantage here is that they are deterministic and
do not confound the memory policy with the reader's reasoning, unlike
LLM-judged accuracy \cite{zheng2023judge}; Section~\ref{sec:endtask} measures
accuracy itself.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
%% ======================================================================

We use LoCoMo \cite{maharana2024locomo}: 10 multi-session conversations, 5{,}882
turns, 1{,}527 scoreable questions. Category-5 (adversarial) items are
excluded --- they carry evidence, but the correct behaviour is to decline, so
retrieving it would not be success.

Retrieval and semantic supersession use all-MiniLM-L6-v2
\cite{reimers2019sbert} at 384 dimensions, and budgets are counted with
\texttt{cl100k\_base}, so a budget is an exact token count. The P2 judge is
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct \cite{qwen2025}, run locally; salience is read as a
continuous expectation over digit-token logits in one forward pass rather than
generated and parsed, which is faster, cannot fail to parse, and leaves no ties
in the eviction ranking.

All intervals are paired bootstrap \cite{efron1993bootstrap} with $B=10{,}000$
resamples drawn at the conversation level, with an exact McNemar test
\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests}. The resampling unit is not a detail: 1{,}527 questions from
10 conversations are not 1{,}527 independent trials
\cite{cameron2008cluster}, and one of our own claims
does not survive the correction (Section~\ref{sec:significance}). Every model
is a pinned local copy with the hub disabled at import, and all numeric tables
are generated from the committed result files rather than transcribed.

\subsection{Storage was never budgeted}
\label{sec:accounting}

Experiments in this area cap the context assembled per query and leave the
store unbounded, and our own experiments did the same until this point. Under
that accounting a store-everything baseline is charged nothing for holding all
419 turns of a conversation, so forgetting can only lose information. Run under
those conditions, our own ablation reported the tiering and compression
machinery as a net loss against simply keeping everything and retrieving, and
that result was correct given how the cost was being counted.

Imposing a storage budget $S$ changes the question from what fits in the
context to what is worth keeping at all. Table~\ref{tab:storage} sweeps $S$
with the context budget fixed at 2048 tokens. The ranking under the corrected
accounting differs from the ranking under the original one. This is the most
consequential observation in the paper, because an evaluation protocol that
omits a cost answers a different question from the one it appears to ask.

\subsection{Selection pays; compression does not}
\label{sec:selection}

Table~\ref{tab:storage} reports both metrics across the sweep. P1-S, which
drops the lowest-utility item when the cap binds, leads on answer recall at
every budget. P1-A, which compresses that item instead of dropping it, leads on
evidence recall at every budget, yet at $S=8192$ trails even RAG on answer
recall, by 3.4 points.

This is the identifier problem of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} in practice. A
demoted gist retains the evidence identifiers of a turn whose text has been
discarded. An evaluation reporting only evidence recall would conclude that
compression is the better use of a bounded store, and an evaluation that checks
whether the answer text survived reaches the opposite conclusion.

\subsection{Statistical significance}
\label{sec:significance}

\subsection{The rate--distortion crossover is not reached}
\label{sec:scaling}

Rate--distortion reasoning predicts compression must eventually beat selection,
since a sufficiently compressed record of everything should outperform a
faithful record of a subset \cite{colaco2026rd}. Sweeping $S$ downward cannot
test this: every policy hits the floor first, near 0.2\% evidence recall at

\subsection{The embedding index is not free}
\label{sec:bytes}

Denominating storage in text tokens leaves out the index that makes retrieval
possible. A 384-dimensional \texttt{float32} vector occupies 1{,}536\,B, while
a LoCoMo turn averages about 130\,B of text. The index therefore outweighs the
content it indexes by roughly a factor of 12, and the limiting resource becomes
the number of stored items rather than their length.

Table~\ref{tab:cost} repeats the sweep with the budget denominated in bytes and
each retrievable item charged for its vector. Two results follow. First, the
selection finding of Section~\ref{sec:selection} is robust to the change of
units, since P1-S leads at every budget above 32\,KiB. Second, below
approximately 40\,KiB an embedding index cannot pay for its own storage: P0,
which maintains no index, performs best. A protocol that counts only text
cannot observe this regime, which is the regime that a memory layer on a
constrained device would occupy.

convey --- its strongest single feature is turn length, ahead of every
hand-designed cue. Under leave-one-conversation-out it never sees the
questions of the conversation it scores, so it is deployable in the way any
supervised component is: fitted offline on labelled conversations, then
applied to new ones. What this does not establish is that the base rates it
absorbs carry over to a different kind of conversation, which
Section~\ref{sec:transfer} tests.

\subsection{A learned scorer across two corpora}
\label{sec:transfer}

LoCoMo's dialogues are generated by an LLM, so a scorer fitted to them may
learn the generator's style rather than what people later ask about. We test
this on REALTALK \cite{lee2025realtalk}: ten conversations between real people
over 21 days, 8{,}944 turns and 660 questions with turn-level evidence in
LoCoMo's schema, of which 160 are answer-recoverable. The scorer is a logistic
regression over 15 shallow features of the message (length, digits, dates,
capitalisation, question and filler cues) concatenated with its 384-dimensional
MiniLM embedding, labelled by whether any question cites the turn as evidence.
Every held-out conversation is scored by three models that never saw it:
trained on the other conversations of the same corpus, on the other corpus
only, or on all 19 other conversations pooled. Policy, budgets and retrieval
are those of Section~\ref{sec:selection} with the fixed heuristic
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}); only the scorer varies.

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports the result. On LoCoMo the pooled scorer
reaches 52.0\% answer recall against the heuristic's 37.5\%, a gain of
$+14.55$ points, and pooling beats same-corpus training by $+3.36$
($[+0.27, +6.52]$), so adding a different corpus helped rather than diluted.
Transfer works in one direction only. Trained on REALTALK alone, the scorer
still beats the heuristic on LoCoMo by $+9.09$; trained on LoCoMo alone, it
falls below the heuristic on REALTALK ($-4.38$, n.s.) and is significantly
worse than same-corpus training there ($-10.62$, $[-18.31, -3.45]$). At this
budget no gain over the heuristic on REALTALK is significant, which with 160
questions in ten clusters is a statement about power as much as about the
scorer.

$S=4096$ is the budget of the headline comparison, not one chosen for this
scorer, so Table~\ref{tab:transfer-budget} repeats the pooled run at four
other budgets. On LoCoMo the gain is significant from $S=2048$ to $16384$ and
largest at $4096$; it shrinks at both ends, where the store holds too little
for any ranking to matter or nearly everything anyway. On REALTALK the gain is
significant at $S=8192$ ($+16.88$, $[+6.31, +26.72]$) and $16384$. The one
budget at which neither corpus shows a significant gain is $S=1024$, about
5\% of a conversation.

The embedding is what carries across. With the hand features alone, the
pooled scorer is significantly \emph{worse} than the heuristic on REALTALK
($-8.75$, $[-17.45, -2.27]$): its feature weights encode LoCoMo's style, such
as long turns ending in a period, which people messaging each other do not
write. This is the same failure the heuristic shows on LongMemEval
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Logistic regression was kept after comparing
it with boosted trees and a 64-unit MLP on the same folds: neither beat it by
more than the spread between held-out conversations, and the trees were much
slower both to fit and on the write path.

\subsection{End-task accuracy}
\label{sec:endtask}

Every result above is context recall, which bounds end-task accuracy without
measuring it. We therefore send each assembled context through one fixed reader,
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct-4bit decoding greedily for at most 32 tokens, and score
the output with the same normaliser the answer-recall metric applies to the
context. ``The answer reached the context'' and ``the answer reached the
output'' are then one predicate evaluated at two points in the pipeline, so the
ceiling comparison is exact rather than approximate. The run covers all 715
answer-recoverable questions with two controls: a closed-book arm that receives
no memory at all, and an oracle arm that receives exactly the cited evidence.

Table~\ref{tab:endtask} reports the outcome. The closed-book floor is 1.7\%,
so parametric knowledge contributes almost nothing on this benchmark and the
margin above that floor is attributable to the memory layer. P1-S reaches
18.5\% against RAG's 12.4\%. Resampling by conversation as before, the realised
gap is $+6.0$ points with a 95\% interval of $[+3.3, +9.1]$, which is 69\% of
the $+8.7$-point recall gap between the same two arms in this pre-fix run
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

Accuracy is not bounded by answer recall, since a reader may guess, so the
table separates the two regimes. Accuracy given that the answer was present is
49.4\% for P1-S against 42.3\% for RAG, while accuracy given that it was absent
is below 1\% for every arm. The selection advantage therefore arrives through
both available routes: P1-S places the answer in context more often, and the
contexts it assembles are also the ones the reader uses more successfully. The
oracle arm converts a complete context into a correct answer 67.3\% of the
time, which bounds what any memory policy can deliver through this reader and
locates the remaining headroom in the reader rather than in the memory layer.

\subsection{Against MemGPT-style and Mem0-style memory}
\label{sec:external}

Every baseline so far is internal. To place MemGate against designs in use,
the memory management of MemGPT \cite{packer2023memgpt} and Mem0
\cite{chhikara2025mem0} was rebuilt inside the same harness, at the same
context budget and store budgets. The MemGPT-style arm keeps a recent queue
and a searchable archive, and folds messages evicted from the archive into a
recursive summary that is always in context and loses its oldest detail
first. The Mem0-style arm writes one compressed fact per turn with ADD,
UPDATE (the nearest stored fact above a similarity threshold is replaced,
newest wins) and NOOP for filler, and evicts the least recently touched fact.
Neither calls an LLM: summaries and facts are written by MemGate's own
extractive compressor, so these are the systems' memory structures rather
than their prompts. In the baselines' favour, each one's settings were
grid-searched on LoCoMo itself at $S=4096$ (MemGPT-style: summary share 0.25,
16-token gists; Mem0-style: 32-token facts, update threshold 0.65). No MemGate
arm was tuned on these answers.

Table~\ref{tab:external} and Fig.~\ref{fig:external} report the result. The
learned scorer beats both rebuilds at every budget, by $+7.3$ to $+24.6$
points over Mem0-style and $+11.5$ to $+22.7$ over MemGPT-style, every gap
significant under clustering. The untrained P1 heuristic beats MemGPT-style
everywhere ($+5.6$ to $+11.3$) but loses to the tuned Mem0-style at $S=4096$,
the budget its settings were chosen on, by $7.0$ points with the interval
excluding zero; it is level at 2048, ahead at 8192 by $+5.2$ (McNemar
$p=0.03$, but not significant once resampled by conversation), and well ahead
at 16384. Mem0-style stops improving at 52.6\% from $S=8192$: UPDATE replaces
a fact with its nearest neighbour, so a threshold loose enough to help at small
$S$ discards distinct facts that merely look alike, and the store never fills.
MemGPT-style stays within about two points of plain RAG, because its summary
holds gists whose answers were compressed away, the failure of
Section~\ref{sec:selection} in a different form.

\subsection{Ten times the training data}
\label{sec:retrain}

The learned scorer that the application first shipped was fitted on 58,616
turns. We retrained it on ten times that: BEAM 1M (74,630 turns) and 10M
(208,696), and MemBench's observation and participation sets, 594,000 turns
in all, with the families of Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss}) weighted equally. Each
candidate was replayed through P1-S on held-out conversations at three to
five budgets per corpus, and the whole protocol was repeated with the folds
dealt from three different orders, 26,444 replays in all. The rule for
shipping a model is that it must lose nowhere.

More data alone did not help (Table~\ref{tab:retrain}, first column). The
10x model gains on MemBench, the corpus that was added, but loses evidence
recall on BEAM 1M by 5.1 to 5.6 points and on BEAM 500K by 3.0 to 5.2, with
the interval excluding zero in every shuffle. Our reading is that a linear
model on 399 inputs was not short of data but of capacity: the new turns are
a different kind of chat, mostly negatives (BEAM 10M adds 208,696 turns and
only a few hundred evidence labels), and they pull the weights toward short
factual statements at the cost of BEAM's long answers. Boosted trees, the
obvious source of capacity, gained on BEAM 500K but lost $8.5$ points on
LoCoMo ($[-11.1,-5.3]$) and were rejected.

Three changes fixed it. The family weight of Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss}) is split
among corpora by evidence turns instead of equally, so BEAM 10M's many
unlabelled turns count for little. Everyday chat is added as a fifth family:
894 long chats assembled from PersonaChat \cite{zhang2018personachat}, 262,876
turns, weakly labelled (a turn is evidence when it states one of its
speaker's persona lines). And the result is averaged in logit space with the
earlier model, Eq.~(\ref{eq:ens}), which keeps the ranking that suited BEAM's
long answers while adding what the new chats taught, at a linear model's
cost. The shipped model is trained on 856,876 turns, 14.6 times the original.
For an everyday-chat test, 50 PersonaChat validation chats that no model saw
are replayed at $S=1024$; each stated persona line is asked as a query, and
the answer is the words of it that the person said. That is a proxy, since
query and evidence share wording, but it treats every arm alike.

At the paper's budgets the shipped model has no significant loss on any
corpus in any of the three shuffles (Table~\ref{tab:retrain}). It gains on
everyday chat ($+7.6$ to $+8.2$ answer points, 69.0\% against 61.4\%), on
MemBench, and on BEAM 100K through the application's own path ($+11.5$ to
$+12.4$ evidence points), and ties on BEAM 1M, where the 10x model lost. Across
all other budgets it has one significant loss: BEAM 1M evidence recall at
$S=8000$, $-1.7$ $[-2.8,-0.6]$, about one cited turn in sixty.

\section{From Harness to Application}
\label{sec:system}
%% ======================================================================

The policy is also a working system. A chat application wraps a reader model
in the same \texttt{observe()} and \texttt{build\_context()} calls the
experiments use (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}): every message, the user's and the
model's reply alike, passes through the policy, and each question is answered
from the context MemGate assembles, never from the raw history. The
application adds one step the experiments do not have, an entry gate that
scores each message the moment it is said. A message the scorer labels filler
(a greeting, ``ok cool'', at most two words), or a model reply scoring below
$0.1$, is forgotten at once and never takes space. Under P1-A and P1-S a
message with $u\ge\tau_{\text{fact}}$ is also written to Tier~3 at once, with
its score, while it stays in Tier~1 for the next few replies; when it later
leaves Tier~1, the near-duplicate rule above folds it into that copy. Every
other message follows the experimental path unchanged. The gate can be
switched off, and the built-in benchmark below runs the policies without it.
The reader is interchangeable because it only ever receives two strings, the
assembled context and the current message. The default runs offline on an
8\,GB laptop with Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \cite{qwen2025}; a hosted model
(Claude, or Gemini \cite{gemini2023} on its free tier) can be substituted, and when a hosted
model is rate-limited and a local copy is installed, Qwen answers that
message instead. With no
model at all the application shows the context the reader would have
received. The application runs P1-S by default, with the shipped learned
scorer of Section~\ref{sec:retrain} inside GuardedScorer
(Eqs.~(\ref{eq:ens})--(\ref{eq:floor}), Fig.~\ref{fig:scoring}); if the MiniLM
embedder is unavailable it falls back to the P1 heuristic rather than scoring
with the wrong features. Any text can be added: pasted text, PDF, Word,
PowerPoint and other files are cleaned, and anything over 120 tokens is cut
into pieces of at most 48 tokens, each scored, stored and retrieved like a
turn, because a single embedding of a long document describes only its
opening.

Two properties follow from reusing the experimental code. First, persistence
is exact: a conversation is saved as its messages and restored by replaying
them through \texttt{observe()}, which is deterministic, so the tiers after a
restart are the tiers before it. Second, the application can check itself:
a built-in benchmark run recomputes the RAG and P1-S arms on all of LoCoMo at
$S=4096$ on a CPU and compares them with the committed results, reproducing
28.1\% and 37.5\% exactly. A side panel shows each message's utility $u$, the
tier it was routed to or the reason it was forgotten, and the exact context
sent to the reader, so every decision the paper measures in aggregate can be
inspected one message at a time. The application runs at the experimental
budgets by default and offers a reduced scale (Tier~1 of four messages, a
512-token context and $S=150$) under which eviction becomes visible within a
few messages; these sizes and the entry gate are the only settings that differ
from the experiments.

reopening a saved million-token chat, which replays it through memory and
takes 70\,s.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
%% ======================================================================

Three errors changed our results before we detected them. Each was caught by an
invariant rather than by reading the code. Two are forms of leakage, a known
cause of irreproducible machine-learning results
\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}.

\emph{A policy that read its evaluation label.} LoCoMo supplies a
\texttt{fact\_id} per evidence turn, and an early consolidation rule promoted
items using it --- the policy was consulting the answer key. The effect was
worth 45 to 47 recall points and invalidated our first reported result. The
remedy is an invariant, not a convention: strip every evaluation label from the
input and the resulting store must be bit-identical. Each decision point added
since was placed under it before any number was reported.

\emph{Component value depends on the current bottleneck.} We first measured the
sentence embedder at $+0.2$ points and concluded it barely mattered. That held
only in the regime measured, where retrieval was saturated against a write path
discarding 93\% of the conversation; once that bottleneck was removed the same
substitution was worth $+19.7$. Ablations run against a bottlenecked pipeline
systematically undervalue whatever would matter after the dominant term moves.

\emph{A failure invisible in the aggregate.} Batched judge scoring returned
\texttt{NaN} for short turns specifically. Precision was the natural hypothesis
and \texttt{float32} did not help; the cause was left-padding, producing fully
masked attention rows. Without an explicit guard every filler turn would have
scored \texttt{NaN} and inverted the eviction order.

A fourth defect was found after the main runs: the heuristic scored each turn
with its speaker tag, which blocked the filler rule and gave nearly every turn
the proper-noun bonus (5{,}863 of 5{,}882 LoCoMo turns were labelled facts).
Recall tables and Table~\ref{tab:transfer} use the fixed scorer; the judge,
end-task, abstractive and LongMemEval runs predate it. The fix moves the
headline P1-S result from 36.8\% to 37.5\%, and it shrinks the learned
scorer's lead over the heuristic at $S=2048$ from $+5.2$ to $+2.7$ points,
because part of that lead was the defect handicapping the heuristic.

%% ======================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%% ======================================================================

\emph{Few conversations.} Clustering by conversation is the correct unit and
leaves few clusters, so intervals are wide; we report the effects that do not
survive as not significant. REALTALK adds ten conversations but only 160
answer-recoverable questions, too few to resolve most of its differences.

\emph{A single reader model.} Section~\ref{sec:endtask} uses one 1.5B four-bit
model \cite{qwen2025} decoding greedily. We claim the ordering of policies, not the absolute
accuracies: the oracle arm's 67.3\% shows much of the remaining error is the
reader, and a larger one would raise every arm and could compress or widen the
margins; a larger reader may also use position in the context differently
\cite{liu2024lostmiddle}.

\emph{Compression was tested in both forms.} Replacing P1-A's word-selection
with a 1.5B model rewriting each evicted turn is worth $+4.06$ answer points
(CI $[+0.84, +6.66]$), so part of the negative result was the compressor rather
than compression. Selection still wins (pre-fix run): 36.8\% against 34.1\%, at 2.1\,s
against 3{,}892\,s and 4{,}879 generations. The claim narrows to: a better
compressor helps, and does not overtake dropping.

\emph{Concatenated streams are synthetic}, as discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:scaling}.

\emph{The scorer is tuned to this corpus.} On a 94-item LongMemEval
\cite{wu2024longmemeval} subsample, whose turns average 210 tokens against
LoCoMo's 32, the heuristic's separation between evidence and non-evidence turns
reversed in sign and scored eviction lost to FIFO. Read the $+9.37$ of
Section~\ref{sec:selection} as what a policy recovers \emph{given a scorer
suited to the corpus}, not as a property of scored selection in general.
The learned scorer has since been tested held out on BEAM, where the heuristic
also ranks evidence below chance, and on MemBench and everyday chat
(Section~\ref{sec:scale}); LongMemEval, where the heuristic's sign first
reversed, is still untested with it, because it could not be fetched in our
environment. Its labels are distant supervision (a turn is positive if
any question cites it), and that supervision is twice as dense on LoCoMo
(24\% of turns) as on REALTALK (12\%); on PersonaChat it is weaker still, a
word-overlap match with a persona line.

\emph{Few answerable questions at scale.} BEAM's answers are mostly
paraphrases, so only 34 to 55 questions per scale are answer-recoverable, and
13 at 10M. The intervals of Table~\ref{tab:scale} are correspondingly wide,
and evidence recall, which covers about ten times as many questions, is
reported alongside. The rebuilt MemGPT-style and Mem0-style arms reproduce
those systems' memory structures without their LLM calls, so they measure the
designs, not the products.

\emph{Single embedder and single judge.} The negative result of
Section~\ref{sec:scorer} is evidence about a 0.5B model prompted per turn, not
about LLM-judged salience in general. A larger judge, or one shown the
neighbouring turns, remains untested and is the most direct way to challenge
the finding.

%% ======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ======================================================================

Under the accounting normally used here, tiered compression is a net loss
against storing everything and retrieving --- but that is an artefact of leaving
the store unbounded. Bound it, and the decision of which items to forget is
worth $+9.37$ answer-recall points at identical storage, fidelity and
retrieval, while compressing an evicted item rather than dropping it loses
$7.13$. The crossover at which rate--distortion theory \cite{cover2006elements} says
compression should begin to pay is not reached to $182\times$. Charging the budget for the embedding index reverses the
ranking below about 40\,KiB. An LLM judge, the obvious route to a better
scorer, loses to a regular expression, for an identifiable reason: salience is
not a property of a turn in isolation. A logistic scorer over a sentence
embedding, trained on LLM-generated and human conversations together, beats
the heuristic by $+14.55$ points on held-out LoCoMo conversations, and by a
significant margin at every store budget from 2048 to 16384 tokens; it
transfers from human dialogue to generated dialogue but not the other way.
Through a fixed reader, $+6.0$ of the pre-fix $+8.7$ points survive as correct
answers. The same scorer beats MemGPT-style and Mem0-style memory, rebuilt in
this harness and tuned in their favour, at every budget tested, and on
held-out chats of up to ten million tokens it beats retrieval on every corpus;
typed into the application, million-token chats keep the answer 51.9\% of the
time against 19.2\%. Ten times the training data did not by itself make a
better linear scorer; weighting corpora by their labels, adding everyday chat
and averaging with the earlier model did, without a significant loss at the
paper's budgets.

The practical recommendation is narrow. Retain a scored subset at full fidelity
rather than compressing everything \cite{jiang2023llmlingua,ge2024icae}, and denominate the budget in bytes with the
index included. The methodological recommendation is broader. Three of the
results above changed in sign or in magnitude once a measurement error was
removed, and none of those errors was visible in the aggregate numbers they
affected.

Two questions remain. The pooled scorer's gains on human conversation are
significant only once the store holds about 40\% of a conversation, and it is
untested on LongMemEval, where hand-crafted
cues inverted; more human-written evidence, rather than a larger model, is
what would settle both. And a scorer learned online from retrieval feedback
is already statistically level with the heuristic on LoCoMo without labels
or future questions ($-2.94$, n.s.); whether it can be seeded with the
pooled scorer and then adapt to each user is the step from a scorer fitted
to benchmarks to one fitted to the person using the application.
